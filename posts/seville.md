Seville is not a subtle city, and that is part of the pleasure. It gives you fountains, palms, warm stone, tiled benches, palace façades, huge plazas, and evening light that makes everything feel a little theatrical.

It is one of those places where the walk itself can become the main attraction. Even before getting to the famous stops, the city already feels generous.

![Fountain and Christmas lights in central Seville](seville5.jpg)

This was a good first note: a fountain, palms, apartment façades, and even the temporary Christmas lights all sharing the same square without making it feel crowded.

![Yellow balconies and orange trees in Seville](seville16.JPG)

Even the ordinary residential corners carry that same brightness. Wooden shutters, iron balconies, orange trees, and hard blue sky make the everyday parts of the city feel just as visual as the famous ones.

## Main Sights

Seville's historic core carries a lot of weight. The Alcázar, the Cathedral, and the General Archive of the Indies are listed together as a UNESCO World Heritage Site, and even if you are not trying to see everything, the city makes it hard not to notice the layers of power, religion, trade, and architecture.

![Pond and pergolas in María Luisa Park](seville6.jpg)

In María Luisa Park, Seville becomes softer. The pond, pergolas, trees, and reflections make the city feel slower after all the hard stone and wide plazas.

![Seville Cathedral in warm evening light](seville7.jpg)

The Cathedral brings back the older, more monumental side of the city. In the low light, the Gothic stonework, orange trees, and lamps all sit in one warm, detailed frame.

![Pergola and pond in María Luisa Park](seville9.jpg)

Back in the park, the mood turns quiet again. The water, tiled path, climbing vines, and ducks make this feel like a pause rather than another sight to tick off.

![Palm trees above an ornate Seville façade](seville13.jpg)

The palms make the skyline feel warm and open, especially when they frame a decorated façade from below.

![Plane trees and palms in a Seville park](seville20.JPG)

The parks also show a softer version of the city: plane trees, palms, clipped greenery, and a pale building partly hidden behind leaves. It gives the architecture a little room to breathe without making the façade the whole subject.

![Metal armillary sphere sculpture near the Guadalquivir](seville15.JPG)

Near the river, the city changes mood again. This metal sphere, the birds perched on it, the grass, and the open sky make Seville feel less enclosed than the historic center.

![Guadalquivir riverbank with trees and a red building in the distance](seville17.JPG)

The Guadalquivir opens the view completely. From here, the city becomes horizontal: river, reflections, trees, moored boats, and a long red building set back on the far bank.

![Wide view along the Guadalquivir in Seville](seville19.JPG)

That distance suits this part of Seville. The riverside trees, the boats, the far bank, and even the small wheel in the distance fold into one long, calm edge of the city.

![Ornate entrance of Palacio de San Telmo](seville18.JPG)

Up close, San Telmo is all ornament, symmetry, flags, stone, and red-and-yellow rhythm. It has the ceremonial confidence that Seville seems to do without much effort.

![Airport bus passing Palacio de San Telmo](seville21.JPG)

Up close, San Telmo turns into a city scene rather than a postcard: the ornate white portal is still there, but the yellow airport bus pulls the palace back into everyday Seville.

## Plaza de España

Plaza de España is one of those places that looks almost unreal in person. Built for the Ibero-American Exposition of 1929, it is grand in a very deliberate way: semi-circular, tiled, symmetrical, and made for slow looking.

![Tiled bridge and canal at Plaza de España](seville2.jpg)

The first view is all scale and detail at once. The tiled bridge in the foreground, the canal, the boats, and the long brick façade all pull the eye across the plaza.

![Plaza de España tower from below](seville14.JPG)

From underneath the arcades, the tower feels even taller. Brick, ceramic bands, balconies, and blue sky stack upward until the plaza turns from a wide space into a vertical one.

![Central building and patterned paving at Plaza de España](seville3.jpg)

The central building is grand, but the patterned paving keeps the foreground busy and human. It is the kind of place where the ground, façades, bridges, and sky all compete for attention.

![Wide view across Plaza de España](seville4.jpg)

From farther back, the plaza reads almost like a stage: towers at the edges, the fountain in the middle, carriages crossing the open space, and a huge amount of blue sky above it.

![Blue-and-white ceramic bridge at Plaza de España](seville8.jpg)

The bridges and water make it feel even more theatrical, but the ceramic railings keep the view close and tactile. It is hard not to stop at every tiled corner.

![Arcaded gallery at Plaza de España](seville10.jpg)

Inside the arcade, the plaza changes again. The curve of the gallery, the coffered ceiling, the columns, and the long shadows make it feel like a promenade rather than only a viewpoint.

![Horse carriage beside Plaza de España](seville12.jpg)

By the end, Plaza de España felt like the most Seville version of Seville: big, warm, decorative, theatrical, and still very much in use, down to the horse carriages waiting beside the canal.

## Las Setas

![Metropol Parasol project at night](seville11.jpg)

Las Setas, officially Metropol Parasol, gives the city a different kind of landmark. Finished in 2011, it is modern, strange, and especially good at night, when the wooden structure becomes more about shape and atmosphere than explanation.

I liked ending with it because it proves Seville is not only its historic image. The city can be ancient, tiled, ceremonial, green, and futuristic-looking within the same trip.
