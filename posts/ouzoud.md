Ouzoud begins at the top, which feels almost unfair. You arrive above the noise before you really understand the size of it, looking down into a canyon where white water drops through red rock, restaurants cling to the slopes, and tiny rafts wait far below like bright little commas in the river.

The waterfalls are one of Morocco's most famous natural places, about 110 meters high and falling in several tiers near the village of Tanaghmeilt in Azilal Province. The route is simple in theory: start high, follow the stairs and dusty paths down to the basin, cross the water, get soaked under the falls, then climb back up through olive trees, people, donkeys, heat, and the constant sound of water behind you.

![A wide first view of Ouzoud Waterfalls dropping into the red canyon with rafts waiting below](ouzoud8.jpg)

From the first viewpoint, Ouzoud looks both open and enclosed. The horizon is wide, but the canyon pulls everything inward: the paths, the terraces, the trees, the people, and finally your attention.

![Sunlight over the red cliff edge above the Ouzoud canyon](ouzoud4.jpg)

Even the bright empty space above the cliffs feels part of the place. The sun is hard, the rock is warm, and the white water waits below like the only cold thing in the whole landscape.

## Down Into The Canyon

The descent starts with steps. Proper steps at first, then uneven stone, then the kind of path where you stop pretending this is just a viewpoint and admit that you are doing a small hike. The waterfall keeps appearing between branches and restaurant roofs, sometimes close enough to fill the frame, sometimes hidden behind a turn.

![Restaurant terraces and paths built into the cliff above the Ouzoud canyon](ouzoud7.jpg)

There is a whole vertical world around the falls. Cafes and restaurants sit on terraces above the river, guides call out to groups, and the path bends around tables, viewpoints, and little pauses of shade.

The name Ouzoud is often linked locally to olives, and it makes sense once you are there. Olive trees follow the path and soften the hard red rock with silver-green leaves. Some explanations also connect the area with old mills near the top of the falls, a reminder that this was never only a pretty viewpoint, but a place shaped by water, trees, work, and village life.

![A tall red canyon wall and blue sky above the Ouzoud valley](ouzoud9.jpg)

There are moments on the way down when the waterfall disappears and the canyon itself takes over. You notice the height of the rock, the sharp light, and how quickly the path moves between shade and glare.

![Clouds moving over the bright sky above Ouzoud](ouzoud10.jpg)

The sky is almost too clean above all that movement. Below, everything is stairs, voices, water, and dust. Above, it is just blue and white, giving the whole walk a strange feeling of heat and suspension.

![The waterfall seen through greenery as the path drops closer to the canyon floor](ouzoud3.jpg)

The lower you go, the louder the water becomes. At the top it is scenery. Halfway down it is weather. By the time you reach the bottom, it has turned into a whole atmosphere.

## At The Bottom

The base of Ouzoud is busy in the best possible way. People cross narrow bridges, wait for boats, take photos, eat by the water, and look up at the falls with the same slightly stunned expression. The canyon does the dramatic part, but the human scale is what makes the place feel alive.

![People, wooden bridges, and small rafts gathered below the falls at Ouzoud](ouzoud13.jpg)

This is where the waterfall stops being distant. The pool is brown and green, the cliffs are warm red, and the rafts nudge each other at the edge of the water while everyone decides how wet they are willing to get.

![Small rafts floating on the river below the red cliffs and falling water at Ouzoud](ouzoud5.jpg)

The raft ride is short, a quick push across the pool rather than a grand expedition, but it is exactly the right amount of chaos. You slide toward the falling water, the sound gets bigger, the air gets colder, and then the spray arrives all at once.

![A raft crossing the pool toward the bright spray of Ouzoud Waterfalls](ouzoud11.jpg)

From the raft, the cliffs feel much taller than they did from the path. The water turns the air silver, and everyone leans into the splash even while pretending they are not about to get completely drenched.

![Cold spray and white water rushing down the rock face at Ouzoud Waterfalls](ouzoud12.jpg)

The water is shockingly cold. Not refreshing in a polite way, more like the canyon has decided to wake you up personally. For a few seconds everything is mist, noise, laughing, wet clothes, and the white wall of the waterfall directly above you.

## Olive Trees And Old Paths

After the raft, the route pulls away from the open basin and back into shade. This is where Ouzoud becomes quieter again. The path passes through olive groves and patches of green that feel almost like a small forest after the glare of the cliffs.

![A shaded olive grove beside the walking path at Ouzoud](ouzoud14.jpg)

It would be tempting to say Ouzoud is the only olive-growing place in Morocco, because the olive trees here feel so essential to the landscape. But that is not true. Morocco has a large olive sector, and olives are grown widely across the country. What makes Ouzoud special is not exclusivity, but concentration: the trees, the old mills, the paths, and the water all sit together in one compact, memorable place.

![Sunlight filtering through the olive trees near the Ouzoud walking trail](ouzoud15.jpg)

The olive groves are also where the pace changes. People stop rushing toward the famous view and start noticing smaller things: dust on shoes, leaves moving above the path, the smell of food from the terraces, and the donkeys working calmly through the crowd.

## The Macaques

Ouzoud is also known for Barbary macaques, the tailless monkeys that live in parts of Morocco and Algeria. They are not just a cute extra for photos. Barbary macaques are the only macaque species native to Africa, and their populations are endangered, which makes seeing them in this landscape feel more meaningful than a quick tourist encounter.

![A Barbary macaque sitting beside the path with green leaves behind it](ouzoud16.jpg)

The macaques move through the area with confidence. They sit on railings, appear in the trees, watch visitors, and sometimes look almost bored by the attention. Their calm can make them seem tame, but they are still wild animals, and the best way to see them is with a little distance and respect.

![Barbary macaques resting on the path near the trees at Ouzoud](ouzoud17.jpg)

They also make the place feel older than the restaurants and viewpoints. The cliffs, the trees, the water, and the macaques all belong to one habitat, and the tourist route is only passing through it.

## The Climb Back Up

The return is where Ouzoud asks for payment. Going down is all views and curiosity. Going up is stairs, heat, wet clothes, and the slow realization that the canyon was much deeper than it looked from above.

![A broad view across the canyon, terraces, paths, and river below Ouzoud Waterfalls](ouzoud6.jpg)

The climb is shared with everyone: families, guides, people stopping to catch their breath, donkeys carrying supplies to the restaurants, and sometimes visitors who have decided that walking back up is no longer part of their personal plan. It is dusty, crowded, funny, tiring, and very Moroccan in its mix of practicality and theater.

![The main waterfall and raft landing seen from above during the climb out of the canyon](ouzoud2.jpg)

Looking back from above, the whole route finally makes sense. The stairs, the rafts, the olive groves, the macaques, the terraces, the cold water, the donkeys, and the red canyon all fold into one loop. Ouzoud is beautiful from the first viewpoint, but it becomes much better when you let it drench you, tire you out, and send you back up the hill with the sound of the falls still stuck in your ears.
